# Seven Polish agglomerations pass, but sharpen the claims

The project's measures are broadly what current research and planning practice would expect, and three of its numbers already agree with independent sources. Scheduled tram speeds fall within about ±1 km/h of the Puls Gdańska timetable ranking for every city. The Clark density gradients for Warszawa and Kraków fall within 0.006 per km of Bertaud and Malpezzi's. The routed times agree with Google Maps to within 2 % in total. The weak points are in how results are summarised and labelled rather than in the routing or the data. The headline transport-performance figure uses a mean of ratios that DG REGIO does not use. The "dense" threshold of 2,500 residents per km² on 250 m cells is lower than any published transit-viability threshold. Compactness (ρ) is still computed on raw GHSL population, which the project itself showed misplaces 16–26 % of people. The transfer layer publishes a random-arrival wait where the literature uses connection waits and generalised time. The implied-slack figure for trams is a model result whose size depends more on how curves and dwell are modelled than on any other assumption. None of these is fatal, and most can be fixed with data the project already holds. The highest-value additions are distribution charts ("share of residents with at least X"), threshold and grid sensitivity shown as ranges, age-group results from the census 1 km grid, a connection-wait and direct-trip view of transfers, and a small real-time check that puts a number on the schedule-versus-reality gap. The literature puts that gap at 5–15 %, which is as large as most of the differences between cities.

## The measures are mainstream; the summaries are where a reviewer would push back

### Accessibility and transport performance follow the field, with three exceptions

The core of the accessibility method is mainstream. It routes with R5 for every departure minute in a two-hour window, takes the median travel time, counts cumulative population, and divides it by the population within a fixed straight-line radius, as DG REGIO and ITF do. Recent comparative work goes further and endorses simple cumulative measures. In Montreal, cumulative counts correlated with gravity measures at above 0.9 for all 24 thresholds tested, peaking at **0.97 when the threshold equalled the regional mean commute time** ([Santana Palacios & El-Geneidy 2022](https://findingspress.org/article/32444)). Across three Canadian metros, simple cumulative measures explained transit mode share better than competition-adjusted (2SFCA-type) measures ([Kapatsila, Negm & El-Geneidy 2026](https://jtlu.org/index.php/jtlu/article/view/2832), abstract only). The r5r authors frame the every-minute window as the answer to the "modifiable temporal unit problem". They note it captures timetable variation but **not deviation from the schedule** ([Pereira et al. 2021](https://findingspress.org/article/21262)). The project's 30- and 45-minute thresholds are also defensible on Polish evidence. Kraków residents' mean trip lasts "almost 30 minutes" ([BIP Kraków KBR 2023](https://www.bip.krakow.pl/?sub_dok_id=184386)). The mean public transport (PT) trip in Gdańsk takes **36 minutes, against 25 by car** ([GBR 2022 Raport 3a, p. 37](https://www.brg.gda.pl/attachments/article/1905/Raport-3a---opracowanie-wynikow-badan-i-pomiarow.pdf)).

> **Correction, 2026-11-24.** This review misdescribes the DG REGIO grid study (Poelman and Ackermans). Read from the paper itself: destinations and the denominator are not limited to the urban centre, the departure window is a 30-minute peak with the shortest of six departures, and a city's value is the mean of cell results. The Polish values (Warszawa, Kraków, Poznań, Łódź) were later read from DG REGIO's public cell map. Under those rules the Polish cities sit near their size-class means; see `results/RAPORT.md` §16.5 and `results/qa/access/gridstudy/gridstudy.md`. Row 7 of the priority table and source 1 of the download list are superseded by that note.

A reviewer would question three choices. The first is the headline aggregation. RAPORT §17 reports the population-weighted **mean of per-cell ratios**, with the ratio of means in brackets. DG REGIO WP 01/2020 reports the ratio of means; the project confirmed this from the annex itself ([DG REGIO 2020](https://ec.europa.eu/regional_policy/sources/work/012020_low_carbon_urban.pdf)). The project's own urban-centre run (§17.5) shows why this matters: the mean of ratios exceeds the ratio of means by up to 9 points in Trójmiasto (41.2 vs 37.1 at 30 min), probably because cells at the edge of a small second centre have small denominators. The second is comparability with the newer DG REGIO grid study. That study covers 197 EU urban centres, uses radii of **8 km and 12 km (16 km/h)**, allows **15 minutes (1.2 km) of walking** and takes **"the best available trip" in the morning peak**. It reports population-weighted means of **36.2 % at 30 min and 53.9 % at 45 min**, and 35.8 % and 59.5 % for urban centres of 1–2.5 million ([Poelman & Ackermans](https://ec.europa.eu/regional_policy/sources/work/assessing_performance_of_urban_public_transport_using_griddata.pdf), partial read). The project's core 30-minute values of 23.8–33.9 sit below those class means. Because the grid study takes the fastest trip and allows a longer walk, its values are higher by construction, so the gap cannot be read as worse service until the project computes a compatible variant. The third is the single threshold pair. The project already stores 5–60-minute thresholds but publishes only 30 and 45.

The project's numbers are of the right order. Excluding GZM, core PT 45 values of 51–59 sit close to the DG REGIO 2020 42-city mean of 57 and to the grid study's 59.5 for 1–2.5 m centres. GZM's 36 sits near the bottom of that class range (37.4), which is consistent with a polycentric network rather than a data error (RAPORT §17.1). The reproduction in DG REGIO's own domain (§16.4) is more thorough than most published comparisons. It isolates the area boundary as the main reason for a 20-point gap, which matches boundary studies showing that tighter boundaries raise absolute accessibility values ([analysis boundaries working paper](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:nex:wpaper:analysisboundaries), abstract only).

### Validation is good on routing and missing on reality

The Google Maps check validates the network build: 28 of 30 comparable pairs fall within ±20 %, and the sum of R5 times is 98 % of the planner's (RAPORT §17.4). It cannot detect schedule optimism, because the planner also routes on timetables. The best-documented bias in this field is exactly that optimism. With real vehicle-location data, accessibility in high-access zones converged **5–15 % below** the schedule-based estimate, and low-access origins were less reliable ([Wessel & Farber 2019](https://jtlu.org/index.php/jtlu/article/view/1502), abstract only). An England-wide study using UK BODS vehicle positions (2025) found observed travel times longer than scheduled in **79.3 % of small areas**, with higher variability in 81.6 % of them ([Chen & Botta 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.11477v1)). Applied to the project's core PT 45 values, 5–15 % means roughly 3–9 points. That is larger than the 3.5-point spread among Kraków, Wrocław, Poznań and Łódź which RAPORT already declines to treat as a ranking, so RAPORT is right to refuse it. The project can now put a number on this for some cities. Kraków (bus and tram) and Gdańsk publish GTFS-RT, as listed on Transitland ([Kraków RT](https://www.transit.land/feeds/f-ztp~krakow~bus~rt); [Gdańsk RT](https://www.transit.land/feeds/f-u3tm-ztm~gdansk~rt)). GZM's real-time data is used by Vooom ([metropoliagzm.pl](https://metropoliagzm.pl/tag/vooom/?print=pdf)). Warszawa's vehicle positions come through a city API with a key ([WarsawGTFS](https://github.com/MKuranowski/WarsawGTFS)). The live status and licences of these feeds were not checked.

Behavioural validation is weak in Poland for a structural reason. Studies in North America find PT accessibility associated with mode share, with logit ρ² ≈ 0.6 in one ([Owen & Levinson 2015](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/transa/v74y2015icp110-122.html), abstract only). The Polish travel surveys (KBR), however, use incompatible definitions: different trip thresholds, walking included or not, and main mode versus any mode used. Only Wrocław 2024 (PT 21.6 % of all trips; [wroclaw.pl](https://www.wroclaw.pl/komunikacja/kompleksowe-badanie-ruchu-2024-we-wroclawiu-i-gminach-sasiednich-wyniki)), Gdańsk 2022/23 and Poznań 2018 come close to comparable. Łódź's survey only began in October 2026, and no GZM household survey was found. With seven cities and three usable surveys, a correlation is not meaningful. Survey shares belong in a context panel, not a validation table.

## Density and form: two external matches, one mislabelled class

### Bertaud's numbers line up once the built-up area is defined the same way

The only harmonised set of dispersion indices and gradients that includes Polish cities is Bertaud and Malpezzi's 48-city study. It gives Warsaw **ρ = 0.99, gradient −0.170 per km** and Cracow **ρ = 1.18, −0.174** (1990s data) ([Bertaud & Malpezzi 2003, Table 1](https://www2.lawrence.edu/fast/finklerm/Complete%20Spatial%20Distribution%20of%20Population%20in%2050%20World%20Ci.pdf)). The project's Clark gradients are 0.1665 and 0.1684 (qa/density/density.md). That they match within 0.006 per km, 25 years apart and from completely different data, is a strong sanity check that RAPORT does not yet cite. The project's ρ for the core at the 10 % threshold is 1.054 for Warszawa and 1.178 for Kraków, close to Bertaud's 0.99 and 1.18. The FUA values at 20 % (1.40 and 1.46, RAPORT §14.3) are higher by construction. Bertaud nets out only *large* non-built areas such as parks, lakes and greenbelts (p. 24). The project's 20 % mask, by contrast, keeps only 46–73 % of FUA population and is star-shaped. RAPORT §14.3 states that ρ "is not comparable" with the published values. That statement should become: comparable when the built-up area is close to Bertaud's definition, and higher by construction otherwise. Bertaud also names Cracow as a city whose central business district (CBD) is eccentric, and says ρ "might have less significance" in such cases (p. 20). That turns the project's untested centre sensitivity (§14.6) from a footnote into a known issue for Kraków.

Angel et al.'s Proximity Index is the closest modern, citable analogue of ρ. It is the mean distance to the centre of an equal-area circle divided by the mean distance from the urban extent to City Hall. It has been measured on satellite-derived footprints for a stratified sample of 200 cities, and no city has a value below 0.35 ([Angel et al., Progress in Planning](https://marroninstitute.nyu.edu/uploads/content/Shape_Compactness_of_Urban_Footprints.pdf)). Its fixed centre rule (City Hall) solves the centre-choice problem in a way reviewers recognise. The Eurostat degree of urbanisation (DEGURBA) classes give a second comparable output. They are defined on 1 km cells: an urban centre has at least 1,500/km² and at least 50,000 people; an urban cluster at least 300/km² and at least 5,000 ([Eurostat DEGURBA manual](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/statistics-explained/index.php/Applying_the_degree_of_urbanisation_manual_-_Methodology_for_applying_level_1_of_the_degree_of_urbanisation_classification), summary only). DEGURBA population shares are directly comparable with OECD's finding that **43 % of Polish FUA residents live in commuting zones, against an OECD average of 25 %** ([OECD, Urban-Rural Linkages in Poland](https://www.oecd.org/regional/urban-rural-linkages-in-poland-94b5c782-en.htm), snippet only).

A reviewer would raise one inconsistency. ρ, the cumulative profiles, the Clark gradient, Gini and η all use raw GHS-POP 100 m, which the project's own check showed places **one person in four to one in six** differently from the census at 1 km (dissimilarity 16–26 %). GHS-POP also puts people in steelworks and ports, and gives large housing estates only 35–60 % of their census population (RAPORT §14.4). Phase 2 moved to a census-rescaled grid; the form measures should follow.

### "Dense" at 2,500 per km² on 250 m cells is a suburban threshold

The density × frequency layer (§19) calls cells of **2,500/km² or more on 250 m cells** "dense". That is 25 residents per hectare. The transit literature's figures are higher, and they are measured on coarser units: about **30 p/ha for basic bus and 50 p/ha for frequent bus** ([Angel, Blei, Parent & Civco 2011](https://www.lincolninst.edu/app/uploads/2024/04/decline-in-transit-sustaining-densities-us-cities_0.pdf)), and about 30 people plus jobs per gross acre (≈ 74 p/ha) around light-rail stations ([Guerra & Cervero 2011, working paper](https://escholarship.org/content/qt3mb598qr/qt3mb598qr.pdf?t=q9uqt6), summaries only). A 250 m cell can be almost entirely residential, so the same population gives it a higher density than a tract average. The project's own data shows the size of this effect: on the same GHSL source, population-weighted density on 250 m cells is about 28–33 % higher than on 1 km cells (density.md). The 2,500 threshold therefore corresponds to roughly 2,000/km² on 1 km cells, near the DEGURBA urban floor rather than any viability level. The "dense, no frequent service" class probably contains many detached-house suburbs, which may overstate the problem in GZM. The same source cautions that "transit service is possible at any density level". It cites Ewing and Cervero's finding that density matters less than proximity to transit and network design (Angel et al. 2011, pp. 191–192). The fix is cheap: rename the class, publish a threshold sweep (1,500 / 2,500 / 5,000 / 7,500 / 10,000), and state that the measure counts residents only, without jobs.

## Transfers: right components, missing time currency

The stop-area transfer layer (§20) breaks a transfer into walk, crossings, steps, step-free path and wait, which matches the literature's structure. Guo and Wilson modelled London transfers per platform pair, and the project's directional variant follows the same logic ([Guo & Wilson 2011](https://www.worldtransitresearch.info/research/3709/), abstract only). Two gaps limit its usefulness.

The first is the time currency. A British meta-analysis values a minute of walking at **1.66 in-vehicle minutes** and a minute of waiting at **1.47** ([Wardman 1998](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/2086/1/ITS195_WP525_uploadable.pdf)). The fixed "pure" transfer penalty is **15.2–17.7 equivalent minutes in Madrid** ([Garcia-Martinez et al. 2018](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/transa/v114y2018ipap52-66.html), abstract only), with a proposed planning range of **13–18** ([Jara-Diaz et al. 2022](https://www.worldtransitresearch.info/research/9198), abstract only). In Dublin, bus-to-bus penalties are 16.5–20.9 minutes, and a dedicated interchange facility was no better than a good ordinary stop. Respondents ranked schedule adherence, real-time information, frequency and shelter above amenities ([Meskell, Brazil & Whitney 2024](https://www.nationaltransport.ie/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/Estimation-of-Bus-to-Bus-Interchange-Penalties-in-an-Irish-Context.pdf)). The newest evidence, from Geneva, finds a residual transfer penalty of only **5–9 minutes** once tram and bus preferences are estimated separately ([Ortelli et al. 2026](https://strc.ch/2026/Ortelli_EtAl.pdf)). Applied to the project's top-20 nodes (walk ≈ 2 min, wait 5–15 min), a generalised transfer time of 1.2–1.7 × walk + 1.5 × wait + 5/13/18 minutes shows that the penalty is about as large as the wait. It also shows that the wait, not the walk, decides the comparison between cities. That sharpens RAPORT's finding that waiting dominates. Weights from published sources make this a transformation rather than a new index, provided it is shown as a band and not used for ranking.

The second gap is the kind of wait. The published wait component is a random-arrival wait, which describes boarding at the start of a trip. A transferring passenger's wait is set by when the feeder line arrives. The project already computes this connection wait inside its coordination test. The test's null result (11–14 % of line pairs "fit", against about 10 % by chance) is what a frequent, headway-based network should produce. Swiss-style pulse timetables are used where services run every 30 minutes, not every 5–10 ([SNAMUTS European monograph draft 2012, p. 20](https://archiv.ivt.ethz.ch/news/archive/20130903_scheurer_SNAMUTS_draft.pdf)). The test should therefore be restricted to pairs where both lines run every 15 minutes or less often, such as suburban bus–rail feeders.

External benchmarks now exist. In the 2015 Warsaw travel survey, **57.2 % of PT trips had no transfer** ([WBR 2015 synthesis, p. 9](https://transport.um.warszawa.pl/documents/62470/8560273/WBR_2015_SYNTEZA_POL.pdf/e845cef6-5f42-76a6-d2b8-50b83e486f2c?t=1634498737045)). Jakdojade's October 2025 route queries give almost the same share for Warszawa, **58 % with no transfer, 33 % with one and 8 % with two**. The no-transfer share is 64–71 % in the other cities, and mean headways run from 12.8 minutes in Wrocław to **25.7 minutes in GZM** ([Jakdojade, Miasta w ruchu 2025](https://www.wroclaw.pl/beta2/files/dokumenty/968637/Jakdojade-raport-2026-Miasta-w-ruchu.pdf)). Jakdojade's figures come from app users, not residents, and several city labels in the PDF text layer are misaligned. They should be used for ordering cities, not for exact levels. For crossings, AMPTI's W6 scale is a published, citable alternative to a bare count. It scores a grade-separated crossing 100 %, signals without turning conflicts 70 %, signals with conflicts 50 %, an unsignalised zebra 30 % and an unmarked crossing 0 %, and OSM crossing tags map onto most of it ([Czekała & Bryniarska 2017](https://yadda.icm.edu.pl/baztech/element/bwmeta1.element.baztech-1b506d14-888a-4ac2-816e-b6d116664788/c/TMiR_6_2017_Czekala.pdf)). The literature on network design adds one strong lay measure. Walker, Mees and SNAMUTS all argue that a connective network needs frequent lines meeting at nodes. The share of a cell's 30-minute reach that needs a transfer, compared with direct trips only, shows that directly. The current transfer-limit test (≤ 0.14 points between ≤ 2 transfers and unlimited) cannot distinguish a mostly direct network from one where a second transfer rarely pays off.

## Speeds validate well; implied slack needs to be shown as a range

The scheduled commercial speeds of trams in RAPORT §15.3 match the only like-for-like Polish benchmark. Puls Gdańska's 2023 timetable ranking gives Kraków 19.3, **Poznań 20.5**, Warszawa 18.9, Gdańsk 17.7, GOP 18.2, Łódź 16.5 and **Wrocław 16.9 (16.2 at peak)** km/h ([Puls Gdańska 2023](https://pulsgdanska.pl/transport/transport-miejski/raport-tramwajowy-2023/EE6rwi8Us9EiYy7r2Tvj)). The project's values (Kraków 19.8, Poznań 19.5, Warszawa 18.6, Gdańsk 18.0, Łódź 16.9, Wrocław 16.3, GZM 18.2 weighted by vehicle-km) differ by no more than 1.0 km/h, and Łódź and Wrocław are the slowest in both. The 2022 edition agrees as closely ([Antyweb summary](https://antyweb.pl/predkosc-tramwajow-w-polsce)). Poznań's 1 km/h gap probably reflects method. Puls takes one trip per line, so the PST line 14 at 27–29 km/h counts in full, and the project's vehicle-km-weighted 20.1 is closer to Puls's figure. As context, French tramways typically run at 17–20 km/h ([SYPTE French comparisons](https://www.urbantransportgroup.org/system/files/general-docs/LRTfrenchcomparisonsreport.pdf), excerpt only), and Polish priority corridors such as Gdańsk's Marynarki Polskiej reach about 27 km/h ([trojmiasto.pl](https://www.trojmiasto.pl/wiadomosci/Tu-tramwaje-jada-najszybciej-Maja-specjalny-priorytet-n228335.html)). The claim in RAPORT §15.3 that *prędkość eksploatacyjna* (operating speed) includes terminal layovers still has no primary citation.

Implied slack is the scheduled time above a kinematic free-running time. It is a transparent method, but no published study found uses it. Published work on schedule padding measures it against an empirical best time from vehicle-location data, not a physics model; one paper locates padding segment by segment by comparing GTFS with real-time data ([Wessel & Widener 2017](https://ideas.repec.org/a/kap/jgeosy/v19y2017i1d10.1007_s10109-016-0244-8.html), abstract only). The project's main parameters are 1.0 m/s² acceleration and braking, a 20 s dwell and a 50 km/h cap (results/qa/speed/stretches/_meta.json). A Tramwaje Śląskie tender specification gives 1.1 and 1.14 m/s² ([Załącznik nr 1 do SIWZ](https://www.tram-silesia.pl/www/wp-content/uploads/2014/05/Zalacznik_nr_1_do_SIWZ.pdf), search excerpt only). With the main parameters, slack is **22 % of scheduled time in Poznań and 35–37 % in Wrocław and Łódź**. The project's sensitivity file shows which assumptions drive this. Acceleration, braking and the speed cap move slack by up to about 7 points (a 40 km/h cap) and leave the ranking almost unchanged (rank correlation ≥ 0.99). A dwell of 10 s or 30 s moves slack from 11–28 % to 33–46 %. Adding a lateral-acceleration limit on curves drops slack to **7–27 %** and changes 1–4 of the top-10 slowest stretches in each city (rank correlation 0.81–0.95; results/qa/speed/stretches/sensitivity_kinematics.csv). The French evidence points the same way. There, dwell was 22–27 % of tram journey time and signal delay 7–8 % ([Wu, McDonald et al.](https://digitalcommons.usf.edu/jpt/vol1/iss2/3/), excerpt only), so a large share of what the model calls "slack" is ordinary dwell and track geometry. Slack therefore needs the label "time above free running (model)", a range rather than a single value, and curve limits derived from OSM geometry before stretches are named as "slow". The Wrocław pattern is strong enough to survive these caveats. Peak speed is 16.0–16.2 km/h against 17.9 after 20:00, and slack is 35 % against 28 % (city_stretch_summary.csv). That time-of-day signature points to traffic and signals rather than geometry, and it is consistent with the city's own statement that tram priority is withheld where simulations show too much loss of junction capacity ([wroclaw.pl](https://www.wroclaw.pl/komunikacja/o-priorytetach-w-komunikacji-i-itsie), excerpt only).

A headline Gini depends heavily on the indicator and assignment method chosen ([Pritchard et al. 2019](https://findingspress.org/article/7412-an-international-comparison-of-equity-in-accessibility-to-jobs-london-sao-paulo-and-the-randstad)). Mode-specific transfer penalties mostly capture a preference for rail over bus, according to Ortelli et al. OSM shelter, display and tactile-paving tags should not be headline values until their coverage has been measured.

## Showing it: one indicator, three linked views, plain-language numbers

The public tools that work best for lay readers pair one simple map per indicator with charts showing who gets access. TransitCenter's dashboard puts charts of access disparities by group next to its maps and keeps the method on a separate "how it works" page ([TransitCenter](https://transitcenter.org/getting-started-with-the-transit-equity-dashboard/)). Access Across America leads with ranked metro tables ([Accessibility Observatory](https://access.umn.edu/ao-research/aaa), search excerpt). CityAccessMap is close to this project in data: a GHSL population grid plus OSM, one city and one indicator at a time ([TU Delft](https://www.tudelft.nl/en/2022/tu-delft/cityaccessmap-addressing-urban-inequalities-with-open-source-data), search excerpt). For seven cities, the chart forms with the best support are ranked dot plots, dumbbells for paired conditions (30 vs 45 min, peak vs evening, core vs urban centre) and the inverted cumulative curve, "% of residents with at least X". That curve removes the arbitrary threshold and compares cities fairly. Radar charts, dual axes, 3D density towers and Lorenz curves as headlines should be avoided. These recommendations rest on standard guides such as the FT Visual Vocabulary, Wilke and the ONS service manual, which were cited from prior knowledge and not reopened.

On a dark basemap the evidence warns against simply reversing colour ramps. In a survey of 214 people, **dark-is-more remained the dominant reading even in dark mode** ([Schiewe 2024](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s42489-024-00171-z), abstract only). An ICA abstract puts that reading at 87–94 % in dark mode against 95–97 % in light mode ([ICA Abstracts 2025](https://ica-abs.copernicus.org/articles/10/251/2025/ica-abs-10-251-2025.pdf), search excerpt). The practical compromise is a perceptually uniform ramp whose high end is the most saturated and contrasting colour, with legends that state direction in words, and a low end clearly distinct from the "no data" hatch and from transparent "no population" cells. Rainbow ramps distort and are unreadable with colour-vision deficiency ([Crameri, Shephard & Heron 2020](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-020-19160-7)). Classed scales with few classes help readers read values ([Datawrapper](https://www.datawrapper.de/blog/classed-vs-unclassed-color-scales)). The project's five named density × frequency classes are therefore better than a 3×3 bivariate grid, for which user-test evidence is thin.

Text matters as much as charts. In a CHI 2024 study, annotations that state a finding made readers rely on hexbin maps **2.86 times more**, and readers drew coarser conclusions from choropleths than from isarithmic maps ([Fan et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.08260v1)). Frequency formats ("4 of 10 residents") reduce denominator neglect across numeracy levels ([Galesic et al. 2009](https://pure.mpg.de/pubman/item/item_2099767_5/component/file_2562291/MG_Using_2009.pdf), abstract only). The interface should give every number a reference point (the seven-city median), one worked example tied to a real place, a caveat marker where a value depends on a data gap or an area choice, and clickable "what stands out" statements that set the map view. Linked selection is the usual way to connect layers. Hovering a city, a legend class or a stop node should highlight it in every view. A shareable URL should carry the city, layer, variant, pinned class and chart selection, extending the project's existing `urlState.js`. Every chart should have a table toggle for accessibility and for journalists ([Equal Entry](https://equalentry.com/accessible-maps-on-the-web/)). For transfers, a node card opened on click, showing a connection matrix of walk plus connection wait and the walking paths with crossings coloured by W6 class, answers "what is it like to change here" better than a coloured dot. That addresses the owner's view that the stop-area layer "adds little". The best open precedent for speed maps is Cal-ITP's: segments coloured by a low-percentile speed for each time period, with a separate layer for variability ([Cal-ITP](https://analysis.dds.dot.ca.gov/rt/README.html)). For a timetable-only project this becomes scheduled speed with fixed breaks plus slack in seconds per km, labelled as modelled.

## Conclusion

The review changes the project's main problem. It is less "are we measuring the right things?" and more "are we saying how sure we are?". On several of the measures a reviewer would check first, independent data from three decades and three methods agrees with the pipeline: Bertaud's density gradients, Puls Gdańska's tram speeds, the Google Maps travel times, and the DG REGIO reproduction in its own domain. That agreement earns the project the right to drop some defensive wording. "ρ not comparable" can become "comparable on a like-for-like built-up area". The schedule-based speed table can be presented with an external validation alongside. The remaining uncertainties are the population grid, schedule optimism and the free-running model. In the middle of the table each is as large as the differences between cities, so they belong next to the numbers as ranges, not in limitation lists.

The project's most distinctive outputs, implied slack and stop-area transfer ease, have no exact precedent in the literature. That makes them valuable, but it also means they need published weights (Wardman, Ortelli, AMPTI W6), model-labelled ranges and one real-time check before anyone outside the project can trust them. A modest GTFS-RT collection in Kraków and Gdańsk would do more for credibility than any new indicator. It would turn the standard caveat "timetable, not reality" into a measured Polish number, which no published Polish study appears to have.
