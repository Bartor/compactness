## Q1. Which public tools are the best references, and what patterns should be copied or avoided?

### Takeaway
The strongest reference tools pair one simple map per indicator with a small number of group/city comparison charts and an explicit "how it works" page; the weakest bury the reader in layer lists. For this project the most transferable patterns are TransitCenter's "chart of disparities next to the map", CityAccessMap's single-city, single-indicator flow with distribution charts, and Our World in Data's "chart + map + table tabs over one indicator with a one-line definition".

### Cited Findings
- TransitCenter Equity Dashboard measures cumulative access within a set travel time to jobs, low-wage jobs and park space, estimated per census block group and summarised per region **by population group**; walking network from OSM, schedules from GTFS; all code published on its "How it works" page — [TransitCenter About](https://dashboard.transitcenter.org/about), [How it Works](https://dashboard.transitcenter.org/methodology) **[abstract only: search excerpts]**; technical documentation (June 2021, Sustainable Systems Research et al.) — [Tech documentation PDF](https://transitcenter.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/07/TransitCenterEquityDashboardTechDocumentation_062421.pdf) **[not opened]**.
- TransitCenter's launch post describes the user-facing components: charts of disparities in access and travel time across race, class and other characteristics; maps of access to jobs and other destinations that can be viewed over time; transit-versus-car travel-time comparison; a fare-affordability view showing how access shrinks without premium fares; demographic layers addable over maps; a video tutorial by the lead developer — [Getting Started With the Transit Equity Dashboard](https://transitcenter.org/getting-started-with-the-transit-equity-dashboard/) **[fetched]**. The page does not document specific controls.
- TransitCenter also produced one-page city "fact sheets" (e.g. Metro New York, Chicago, DC) as printable summaries alongside the dashboard — [NYC Fact Sheet](https://transitcenter.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/06/NYCFactSheet.pdf), [Chicago Fact Sheet](https://transitcenter.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/06/ChicagoFactSheet.pdf) **[listed in search, not opened]**. Pattern: a static per-city summary distilled from the interactive tool.
- University of Minnesota Accessibility Observatory, "Access Across America": annual report series since 2015 on job access by car, transit, walking and cycling for large US metros; latest summary January 2026 (2024 data), with a "ten years of measurement" retrospective; the Observatory lists an interactive map of its 2022 findings — [Accessibility Observatory](https://access.umn.edu/ao-research/aaa), [Ten years of measurement (Jan 2026)](https://access.umn.edu/news-pubs/news/2026/january/access), [Return of congestion… (Oct 2024)](https://www.cts.umn.edu/news-pubs/news/2024/october/accessibility) **[search excerpts]**. Its main public format is ranked metro tables plus maps, i.e. a league table is the headline product.
- CityAccessMap (Leonardo Nicoletti, Mikhail Sirenko, Trivik Verma, TU Delft): web app showing urban accessibility for nearly every city of 100,000+ inhabitants worldwide, from OSM amenities and the EU JRC Global Human Settlement Layer population grid; built with Svelte, Mapbox and D3; tied to the paper "Disadvantaged communities have lower access to urban infrastructure" (Environment and Planning B, DOI 10.1177/23998083221131044, 50+ amenity types); intended for planners in less well-funded cities — [TU Delft news](https://www.tudelft.nl/en/2022/tu-delft/cityaccessmap-addressing-urban-inequalities-with-open-source-data), [Information is Beautiful Awards entry](https://informationisbeautifulawards.com/showcase/5302-cityaccessmap) **[search excerpts]**. Note: data model (population grid + walking time) is very close to this project's.
- Datawrapper reports that it makes maps accessible by providing alternative descriptions and data tables — [Datawrapper: how we make charts, maps and tables accessible](https://www.datawrapper.de/docs/how-we-make-sure-our-charts-maps-and-tables-are-accessible) **[listed, not opened]**.
- Our World in Data "Grapher": every indicator page offers Chart / Map / Table tabs over the same data, a one-line subtitle defining the indicator, a "Sources and processing" block below, entity selection that persists across tabs, and a full shareable URL state — [OWID Grapher (example page)](https://ourworldindata.org/grapher/life-expectancy) **[known, not opened]**.
- Atlas of Urban Expansion (NYU Marron / Lincoln Institute): per-city pages with a small set of standardised metrics, maps of built-up area over time and comparison tables across cities — [atlasofurbanexpansion.org](http://atlasofurbanexpansion.org/) **[known, not opened]**. JRC Urban Data Platform Plus — per-city dashboards with indicator panels and benchmark against national/EU averages — [urban.jrc.ec.europa.eu](https://urban.jrc.ec.europa.eu/) **[known, not opened]**. OECD Regions and Cities "Regional Well-Being" — each region scored per dimension with its rank shown as a position on a dot strip among all regions — [oecdregionalwellbeing.org](https://www.oecdregionalwellbeing.org/) **[known, not opened]**.
- Transit frequency maps: Jarrett Walker's "frequent network" concept — maps that show only service at or better than a headway threshold (typically 15 min), because frequency is "freedom" for riders — [Human Transit: frequent network maps](https://humantransit.org/2010/08/basics-the-spontaneity-test.html) **[known, not opened]**. This is the conceptual basis of the project's headway-class layer.

### Inferences
- The two highest-value patterns to copy: (1) TransitCenter-style **"who gets access" distribution charts** next to the map (share of population above a threshold, per city), and (2) OWID-style **one indicator, three views (map / chart / table) with shared selection and URL**.
- OECD Regional Well-Being's "position on a strip of all peers" is the simplest lay-reader benchmark and maps directly onto the existing all-city table (a dot strip per metric).
- Avoid copying the layer-catalogue style of planner dashboards (many checkboxes, one legend per layer): it is exactly the owner's earlier complaint.

- No source on 15-minute-city atlases was found ("CityAccessMap" is the closest).

## Q2. What does research and guidance say about map colour and classing (dark basemaps, palettes, bivariate, classes, missing data, labels)?

### Takeaway
On dark backgrounds, readers still mostly assume **dark = more**, so simply reversing a ramp ("light = more") is not safe by default; classed scales with few classes are better for reading values, unclassed for patterns; perceptually uniform, colour-blind-safe ramps are required; bivariate maps cost reader effort and are poorly evidenced.

### Cited Findings
- Schiewe (2024), "Dark-is-More Bias Also in Dark Mode? Perception of Colours in Choropleth Maps in Dark Mode", KN – Journal of Cartography and Geographic Information 74(2):171–180: online survey of 214 people; the dark-is-more interpretation remained the most common even in dark mode, slightly weaker than in light mode, and dependent on the particular colour scheme and on user expertise — [HCU repository record](https://repos.hcu-hamburg.de/handle/hcu/1037), [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s42489-024-00171-z) **[abstract only; PDF downloaded but text not extractable here]**.
- An ICA abstract (2025) reports that, depending on scheme, 87.1–93.5 % of participants chose dark-is-more in dark mode versus 95.1–97.0 % in light mode — [ICA Abstracts 10, 251, 2025](https://ica-abs.copernicus.org/articles/10/251/2025/ica-abs-10-251-2025.pdf) **[search excerpt]**. A 2026 ICA abstract summarises Schiewe's work as finding light-is-more acceptable for some ordinal scales (e.g. air quality) — [ICA Abstracts 12, 49, 2026](https://ica-abs.copernicus.org/articles/12/49/2026/ica-abs-12-49-2026.pdf) **[search excerpt; conflicts somewhat with the headline result—verify]**.
- Esri practitioner guidance: reversing a ramp on a dark basemap works well for graduated/proportional symbols because the basemap stays visible, but "if you are making a filled-polygon map, be careful"; a reversed palette needs the dark background visible as a cue, so show part of the basemap in the default extent; for diverging maps on a dark basemap the centre of the ramp should be dark — [Esri: dark basemaps](https://www.esri.com/arcgis-blog/products/arcgis-online/mapping/dark-basemaps), [Esri: light and dark color schemes](https://www.esri.com/arcgis-blog/products/arcgis-online/mapping/light-and-dark-color-schemes) **[search excerpts]**. CARTO: on dark backgrounds consider flipping the order, and update the legend — [CARTO cartographic tips](https://carto.com/blog/cartography-data-extract-value/) **[search excerpt]**.
- Crameri, Shephard & Heron (2020), "The misuse of colour in science communication", Nature Communications: rainbow-like and red–green maps visually distort data through uneven gradients (bright yellow gets outsized weight) and are unreadable to readers with colour-vision deficiency; call for scientifically derived, perceptually uniform maps (free set at fabiocrameri.ch/colourmaps) — [Nature Communications](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-020-19160-7), [PMC copy](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7595127/) **[search excerpts]**. Crameri's set includes "batlow", and sequential maps such as "lajolla"/"bilbao" that are designed to end dark or light — [Scientific colour maps](https://www.fabiocrameri.ch/colourmaps/) **[known, not opened]**.
- Datawrapper (Lisa Charlotte Muth), classed vs unclassed: use classed scales for ordinal data and statistical brackets; unclassed to show general patterns, outliers and smooth/abrupt transitions; "more classes mean more nuance but harder value reading"; with few classes readers read values best (Mak & Coulson 1991 found a significant advantage of classed maps in value-estimation tasks); Tyner (2010) calls unclassed "the most exact representation"; Dobson (1980) calls classed maps a "simpler, more efficient communication device"; legend design can flip which is easier to read; recommended process: start unclassed, then decide whether to simplify — [Datawrapper blog](https://www.datawrapper.de/blog/classed-vs-unclassed-color-scales) **[fetched]**.
- data.europa.eu visualisation guide has a page on colour scales for numerical data — [data.europa.eu guide](https://data.europa.eu/apps/data-visualisation-guide/colour-for-numerical-data-colour-scales) **[listed, not opened]**.
- ColorBrewer (Brewer & Harrower) provides sequential schemes of 3–9 classes, flags colour-blind-safe and print-safe schemes, and is the de facto standard for classed choropleths — [colorbrewer2.org](https://colorbrewer2.org/) **[known, not opened]**. The common practitioner rule of thumb is 5–7 classes for choropleths (Slocum et al., Thematic Cartography and Geovisualization) **[known, not opened; book]**.
- Bivariate maps: user-testing evidence is sparse ("the paucity of user-based, comparative testing of bivariate map types presents a significant problem") — [ICC 2013 extended abstract](https://icaci.org/files/documents/ICC_proceedings/ICC2013/_extendedAbstract/278_proceeding.pdf) **[search excerpt]**. Poorly chosen bivariate methods force readers to consult the legend more often (Cartographic Perspectives, 2019) — [Cartographic Perspectives](https://cartographicperspectives.org/index.php/journal/article/download/1538/1817/9315) **[search excerpt]**; a PLOS ONE study found participants spent significantly more time on the legend with an "intrinsic" bivariate encoding — [PLOS ONE 10.1371/journal.pone.0250164](https://doi.org/10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0250164) **[search excerpt]**.
- How lay readers form takeaways: CHI 2024 study (Fan, Lei, Mancenido, Maciejewski, MacEachren; 103 participants, 4,644 written takeaways) — readers were about 10.7× more likely to give coarser (larger-region) takeaways from choropleth maps than from isarithmic maps; annotations that state facts increased reliance on the map for hexbin maps 2.86× — [arXiv 2403.08260](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.08260v1) **[fetched]**.

### Inferences
- Dark theme: keep the **dark-is-more convention as the default for value layers is not possible literally** (dark cells vanish into a dark basemap), so the practical compromise is a ramp whose **high end is the most saturated and most contrasting colour** (e.g. dim desaturated blue-grey → bright saturated yellow/orange, as in viridis/inferno-like ramps), plus a legend that is always explicit with "more →". Given Schiewe's finding, the legend must label direction in words ("więcej ludzi", "częściej"), not rely on lightness.
- The low end should not be near-black: low-but-present values must be distinguishable from "no data" and from "no population" (transparent). Use the hatch only for "no timetable" and transparent for "nobody lives here".
- Density (250 m cells, heavy-tailed) suits **classed, quantile-like or hand-chosen breaks with 5–6 classes** and round thresholds people can repeat; performance share (0–100 %) suits 5 classes at round percentages; headway classes are ordinal and must stay classed.
- The five-class density × frequency layer is effectively a bivariate map collapsed to named categories — this is the better form for lay readers than a 3×3 colour grid, provided each class has a plain name and the legend shows the 2-D logic as a small matrix.
- Labels: put place labels and major roads **above** data layers (MapLibre layer order) with halo, and keep data at partial opacity at low zoom; this is standard practice in vector basemaps but was not sourced here.

- Could not read Schiewe's explicit design recommendations for dark-mode schemes (PDF text extraction failed: no PDF tools installed). The report writer should check the full text.
- No empirical source found on hatching for missing data (practitioner convention only).
- No study found on number of classes readable on dark backgrounds specifically.

## Q3. Which chart types work best for city comparisons and distributions for lay readers, and which to avoid?

### Takeaway
For a lay audience comparing seven cities, ranked dot plots / bars, slope or dumbbell charts for two conditions, and "share of people with at least X" curves are the best-supported forms; radar charts, dual axes and 3D should be avoided. Distribution views (strips, Lorenz) are useful but need annotation.

### Cited Findings
- Financial Times Visual Vocabulary groups charts by the relationship to show (deviation, correlation, ranking, distribution, change over time, magnitude, part-to-whole, spatial, flow); "Ranking" recommends ordered bars, ordered columns, dot strip plots, slope charts and lollipops; "Distribution" recommends histograms, dot plots, dot strips, barcode plots, boxplots, violin, population pyramid, cumulative curve, frequency polygons, beeswarm — [FT Visual Vocabulary (GitHub)](https://github.com/Financial-Times/chart-doctor/tree/main/visual-vocabulary) **[known, not opened]**.
- Wilke, *Fundamentals of Data Visualization*: recommends dot plots over bars when values do not start from a meaningful zero; strip charts/sina/violin for many-distribution comparisons; ECDFs (cumulative distributions) for showing "proportion with at least X"; warns against dual axes and 3D ("avoid 3D … unless it maps a real third dimension") — [clauswilke.com/dataviz](https://clauswilke.com/dataviz/) chapters "Visualizing distributions" and "Don't go 3D" **[known, not opened]**.
- UK ONS Service Manual data-visualisation guidance: prefer bar charts and line charts; avoid pie and 3D charts; keep charts simple, label directly, write a title that states the main message — [ONS Service Manual: data visualisation](https://service-manual.ons.gov.uk/data-visualisation) **[known, not opened]**.
- Stephen Few, "Keep Radar Graphs Below the Radar": radar charts make comparisons of values and shapes hard and depend on axis order — [Perceptual Edge PDF](https://www.perceptualedge.com/articles/dmreview/radar_graphs.pdf) **[known, not opened]**.
- Risk/number communication evidence relevant to "how many people have at least X": icon arrays improved accuracy for both low- and high-numeracy people and eliminated denominator neglect, strongest for low numeracy (Galesic, Garcia-Retamero & Gigerenzer 2009; Garcia-Retamero & Galesic 2010) — [Galesic et al. 2009 (MPG PDF)](https://pure.mpg.de/pubman/item/item_2099767_5/component/file_2562291/MG_Using_2009.pdf), [Garcia-Retamero, Okan & Cokely 2012 review (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3354448) **[abstract only]**; but visual aids confused some older adults with low numeracy and low graph literacy (Garcia-Retamero & Cokely 2013) — [Garcia-Retamero & Cokely 2013 (MPG PDF)](https://pure.mpg.de/pubman/item/item_2098431_7/component/file_3569483/Garcia_Communicating_2013_VerlPDF_final_LS.pdf) **[abstract only]**.

### Inferences (own design judgement)
- **Ranked dot plot (one row per city, one dot per metric condition)** is the best default for the all-city view: 7 rows fit on mobile; values like 38 % vs 52 % need no zero baseline.
- **Dumbbell / slope chart** for paired conditions: 30 vs 45 min, peak vs evening, administrative city vs urban centre. A dumbbell (two dots joined, cities ranked) reads better than a slope chart once lines cross a lot; with 7 cities either works.
- **Cumulative "people with at least X" curve** (an inverted ECDF of persons, x = reach share or headway, y = % of residents) is the single most informative chart for this data: it answers "how many people have good service" at any threshold, and small multiples or overlaid lines per city compare cities fairly. Highlight the threshold used on the map with a vertical rule linked to the legend.
- **Stacked 100 % bar of population by class** (density × frequency classes or headway classes, per city) doubles as a legend and a city comparison; this is probably what `classShares.js` already computes.
- **Scatter density vs service** at cell level: useful for analysts but heavy for lay readers; offer as a per-city view with hexbin/2-D density, not 100k points; annotate quadrants with the same five class names used on the map.
- **Lorenz curve / Gini**: avoid as a headline — needs explanation and the "share of people with at least X" curve carries the same information more readably.
- **Time-of-day profile**: line chart with time on x for a handful of cities, or small multiples; never dual axes.
- **Avoid**: radar charts for the ~25-metric city profile (use a dot strip per metric instead), 3D, dual axes, pies with more than 2–3 parts.

### Gaps
- FT, Wilke, ONS and Few pages were not reopened this session; quotes above are paraphrases.
- No controlled study found comparing slope vs dumbbell charts for lay readers.

## Q4. How should indicators be explained in the interface?

### Takeaway
Lead with a one-sentence plain-language reading of the number ("3 of 10 residents can…"), then a reference point, then the method behind a disclosure; frequency formats ("x of 10 people") and annotations that state the finding increase comprehension, especially for low-numeracy readers.

### Cited Findings
- Frequency/icon formats reduce denominator neglect and improve accuracy across numeracy levels — [Galesic et al. 2009](https://pure.mpg.de/pubman/item/item_2099767_5/component/file_2562291/MG_Using_2009.pdf), [Garcia-Retamero, Okan & Cokely 2012 (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3354448) **[abstract only]**; the benefit of natural frequencies for public understanding is contested in some studies (e.g. a 2016 study titled "Natural frequencies do not foster public understanding of medical test results"; McDowell & Jacobs 2017 meta-analysis) — cited in [Judgment and Decision Making 2022](https://dlab.sauder.ubc.ca/sjdm/journal/21/210615/jdm210615.html) **[search excerpt]**.
- Text that states findings changes what readers take away from maps: in the CHI 2024 study, perceiver-independent annotations increased reliance on hexbin maps 2.86× and aligned text shifted takeaways to higher semantic levels — [arXiv 2403.08260](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.08260v1) **[fetched]**.
- TransitCenter explains its dashboard with a separate "How it works" page plus a video tutorial and a podcast, i.e. explanation is layered outside the main view — [Getting started](https://transitcenter.org/getting-started-with-the-transit-equity-dashboard/) **[fetched]**.
- Datawrapper: classed legends are better when readers must read values; the legend design is a separate, decisive factor — [Datawrapper blog](https://www.datawrapper.de/blog/classed-vs-unclassed-color-scales) **[fetched]**.
- ONS guidance: chart titles should state the main point; subtitles define the measure; notes give source and caveats — [ONS Service Manual](https://service-manual.ons.gov.uk/data-visualisation) **[known, not opened]**. Our World in Data puts a one-sentence definition under each chart and full method in "Sources and processing" below — [OWID](https://ourworldindata.org/) **[known, not opened]**.

### Inferences
- The project's current "how to read first, how computed second" order matches the evidence; extend it with: (1) a **worked example tied to a real place** ("w komórce przy Rondzie X mieszka 2 400 osób; w 30 min dojedziesz do 41 % mieszkańców w promieniu…"), (2) **frequency phrasing** ("4 na 10 mieszkańców") for shares, (3) a **reference point** (median of the seven agglomerations, or the best city) in every number cell.
- Caveats need their own visible marker (e.g. a small "!" chip) when a value depends on a timetable gap or on an area definition choice; the no-data hatch should be explained in the legend, not only in the About page.
- "What stands out" statements are an evidence-backed pattern (annotations increase map reliance) — make each statement clickable to set the map view/layer it refers to.

### Gaps
- No transport-specific comprehension study (how lay readers understand "share of nearby population reachable in 30 minutes") was found; worth a small hallway test.

## Q5. Linked views and interaction: what works, what fails?

### Takeaway
Brushing/linking between legend, map, table and chart is the main way to "connect layers and views"; the documented failures of map interfaces are too many widgets, legends that hide the map, and unstructured exploration with no guided entry point.

### Cited Findings
- Roth & Harrower (2008), usability study of the Lakeshore Nature Preserve interactive map: design the interface around the mapped data, reuse familiar conventions, and avoid pitfalls such as overly minimalist widgets — [Cartographic Perspectives 60](https://cartographicperspectives.org/index.php/journal/article/view/cp60-roth-harrower), [PDF](https://geography.wisc.edu/cartography/projects/publications/RothHarrower_2008_CP.pdf) **[search excerpts]**.
- Roth (2011): map symbols can be presented in an expandable interactive legend; most people use interactive maps in an unstructured way — [ICC 2011 poster](https://geography.wisc.edu/cartography/projects/publications/Roth_2011_ICCposter.pdf) **[search excerpt]**. Roth et al. (2017) review of user studies in cartography notes the need for more research on interactive maps — [User Studies in Cartography (CORE)](https://core.ac.uk/download/154256558.pdf) **[search excerpt]**.
- Shneiderman's "overview first, zoom and filter, then details on demand" mantra (1996) is the standard framing for this kind of tool — [Shneiderman 1996, "The Eyes Have It" (UMD tech report)](https://www.cs.umd.edu/~ben/papers/Shneiderman1996eyes.pdf) **[known, not opened]**.
- Accessible-map guidance: the main recommendation is a synchronised data table or list as an alternative to the map — [Equal Entry: Accessible maps on the web](https://equalentry.com/accessible-maps-on-the-web/), [Minnesota IT interactive maps quick card](https://mn.gov/mnit/assets/InteractiveMapsQuickCard_tcm38-725698.pdf) **[search excerpts]**.

### Inferences (design judgement)
- One selection state shared by all views: hovering a city row highlights its outline and its dot in every chart; hovering a legend class highlights cells on the map **and** the matching segment in the class-share bar; clicking a stop node scrolls the table/panel to it.
- Keep the exclusive/combinable distinction visually unmistakable: radio cards for value layers, toggles for overlays, and never more than one legend for value layers visible at a time.
- Persist selection (city, layer, pinned class, variant, map view) in the URL — the project already has `urlState.js`; extend it to chart selections and pinned classes so a journalist can share an exact view.
- Provide a guided entry: a default "story" view per city (one recommended layer + 2–3 "what stands out" chips) to counter unstructured exploration.

### Gaps
- No quantitative study on legend-over-map occlusion or radio/checkbox mode confusion in map dashboards was found in this session.

## Q6. Performance and technical patterns for this stack (MapLibre, PMTiles, React)

### Takeaway
Vector tiles + `feature-state` for hover/highlight + filter expressions for class pinning are the standard performant pattern; accessibility requires a parallel HTML table and keyboard-reachable lists, since map canvases are not navigable by screen readers.

### Cited Findings
- MapLibre GL JS supports `setFeatureState` and `feature-state` expressions to restyle features (hover, selection) without re-uploading data; requires feature ids (`promoteId` for vector sources) — [MapLibre style spec: expressions (feature-state)](https://maplibre.org/maplibre-style-spec/expressions/#feature-state), [MapLibre example: hover effect](https://maplibre.org/maplibre-gl-js/docs/examples/create-a-hover-effect/) **[known, not opened]**.
- PMTiles is a single-file tile archive readable via HTTP range requests, suitable for static hosting such as GitHub Pages — [Protomaps PMTiles docs](https://docs.protomaps.com/pmtiles/) **[known, not opened]**.
- Web map keyboard support is often limited to pan/zoom; keyboard users frequently cannot focus map features; popups must be keyboard- and screen-reader-accessible with a labelled close button; some SDKs announce map moves via screen-reader descriptions (Azure Maps) or render a hidden table (ApexMaps) — [WebAIM discussion](https://webaim.org/discussion/mail_message?id=50894), [Maps accessibility best practices](https://mgifford.github.io/ACCESSIBILITY.md/examples/MAPS_ACCESSIBILITY_BEST_PRACTICES.html), [Azure Maps accessibility](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-au/azure/azure-maps/map-accessibility) **[search excerpts]**.
- WCAG 2.2 requirements most relevant here: 1.4.1 Use of Color (colour not the only means), 1.4.11 Non-text Contrast 3:1 for graphical objects and UI components, 2.4.7/2.4.11 focus visible/not obscured, 2.5.8 target size minimum 24×24 CSS px — [WCAG 2.2](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/) **[known, not opened]**.
- Sortable tables: the WAI-ARIA Authoring Practices "Sortable Table" pattern uses `aria-sort` on the column header and buttons inside headers — [WAI-ARIA APG sortable table](https://www.w3.org/WAI/ARIA/apg/patterns/table/examples/sortable-table/) **[known, not opened]**.

### Inferences
- Legend hover/pin → use a `filter` or `feature-state`-free paint expression (`case` on the class attribute vs a pinned value) to dim non-matching cells; this avoids per-feature state on hundreds of thousands of cells.
- Stop nodes: enlarge hit areas with an invisible wider circle layer; WCAG 2.5.8 target size suggests ≥24 px effective target.
- Mobile: map on top, bottom sheet for sidebar; tables become per-city cards with the metric dot strip, rather than wide horizontal tables.
- Each chart should have a "show as table" toggle — satisfies both accessibility and journalists who want numbers.

### Gaps
- MapLibre-specific accessibility features (e.g. any built-in keyboard feature navigation as of late 2026) were not verified.

## Q7 (objective c). Prioritised list of what this project should add or change

Ranking = expected value for a lay reader ÷ effort (own judgement, using findings above). H/M/L = effort.

1. **"How many people have at least X" curve per city (inverted cumulative distribution), with the map's class thresholds marked** — answers the core question of the transport-performance and headway layers in one chart; directly comparable across cities; can show 30 vs 45 min as two lines. Effort M. Example: Wilke ECDF chapter ([clauswilke.com/dataviz](https://clauswilke.com/dataviz/)); TransitCenter's group-access charts ([dashboard](https://dashboard.transitcenter.org/)).
2. **Population-by-class 100 % bar per city that is also the legend** (for density × frequency classes and headway classes); hover/pin a segment highlights the class on the map. Delivers the owner's wish for "connections between layers and views" with data already computed. Effort L–M. Example: FT Visual Vocabulary part-to-whole ([GitHub](https://github.com/Financial-Times/chart-doctor/tree/main/visual-vocabulary)).
3. **Ranked dot strip per metric in the all-city table** (each cell or row shows a mini dot strip of all seven cities with the current city highlighted, plus the median). Gives every number a reference point. Effort L. Example: OECD Regional Well-Being ([oecdregionalwellbeing.org](https://www.oecdregionalwellbeing.org/)).
4. **Dumbbell charts for paired variants** (30 vs 45 min; peak vs evening; city vs urban centre). Effort L. Example: FT "ranking/change" categories.
5. **Fix the dark-theme ramps**: perceptually uniform, colour-blind-safe sequential ramps whose high end is brightest/most saturated, low end clearly above the basemap, legend labelled with words for direction; hatch only for missing timetables; transparent for no population. Check with a CVD simulator. Effort L. Sources: Schiewe 2024; Crameri 2020; Esri dark basemap guidance.
6. **Linked selection everywhere + URL state** (city, layer, variant, pinned class, chart selection) and clickable "what stands out" statements that set the view. Effort M. Sources: Shneiderman; Roth 2011; OWID shareable grapher URLs.
7. **Frequency phrasing and worked examples in "?" explanations** ("4 na 10 mieszkańców", one real-place example per indicator), and caveat chips. Effort L. Sources: Galesic et al. 2009; CHI 2024 annotations.
8. **Time-of-day profile** (line chart, small multiples per city) once time variants are on the site; links to a time selector on the map. Effort M.
9. **Speed overlay for lines/stretches**: single-hue or viridis-like ramp on line width+colour, classed in round km/h bands (e.g. <12, 12–16, 16–20, >20), with a ranked bar of slowest stretches that zooms the map on click. Effort M–H.
10. **Per-node detail and "reach that needs a transfer" layer**: show only on demand (click), with a small chart in the panel (e.g. transfer walk vs wait as a dumbbell) rather than another always-on coloured layer — addresses the owner's view that the stop-area layer added little. Effort M.
11. **Accessibility pass**: every chart has a table toggle; sortable tables with `aria-sort`; keyboard-reachable list of cities/nodes synced with the map; contrast 3:1 for legend swatches and hatch on dark. Effort M. Sources: WCAG 2.2, WAI-ARIA APG, Equal Entry.
12. **Per-city printable/shareable summary card** (TransitCenter fact-sheet style) for journalists and officials. Effort M–H. Example: [TransitCenter NYC fact sheet](https://transitcenter.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/06/NYCFactSheet.pdf).

Avoid: radar charts for city profiles, a 3×3 bivariate colour grid (keep named classes), coloured city backdrops, multiple simultaneous value legends, cell-level scatter as a default view, Lorenz curves as headline charts.
